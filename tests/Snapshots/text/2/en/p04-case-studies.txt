The facts that changed how a case was understood
A signature on a cheque, information missing from a car sale, a name appearing in a transaction under investigation. In each of these matters, the facts needed to be considered together: what happened, what the documents established, and where the real dispute lay.
Here we present examples of matters we have worked on, showing how evidence was organised, material issues examined, and what the work described achieved. Identifying details have been removed or condensed to protect client confidentiality. Outcomes depend on the facts, documents and stage of proceedings. A previous outcome does not guarantee a similar result in another matter.

A signed blank cheque did not tell the whole story
Debts and Cheques
Matter: cheques, a financial claim and earlier judgments
A businessman left a cheque signed in blank, then found himself facing a financial claim from someone he had trusted. According to the case summary, the cheque was used after leaving his possession, and his signature became central to the dispute.
By the time the matter reached Summit, earlier judgments had been issued and had financial consequences for the client. The documents before him appeared decisive: a cheque bearing his signature, existing judgments and money that had already left his assets. What needed to be established was the available route for challenging that outcome, and what the file could add to the material already presented.
How the cheque became a claim
The review began with the circumstances in which the cheque left its owner's possession, how it was used, and what had been submitted about it at each stage. It included the earlier judgments and their reasoning, the documents supporting the claim, and the defences and responses already presented.
The signature was a fact that had to be addressed directly. This required an account of the parties' relationship, the sequence of dealings, correspondence and payments, together with an examination of how the criminal proceedings affected the civil claim. Leaving out any of these elements would have left the account incomplete.
What changed in the presentation of the case
The facts and documents were reorganised, with each point linked to its supporting evidence. The work focused on the circumstances in which the cheque was used, the consistency of the opposing account, and the material issues requiring clearer presentation at the challenge stage.
This approach allowed the dispute to be presented in its full context, distinguishing what the documents actually established from what still required proof. Simply repeating an objection to the earlier outcome was insufficient; the basis on which it rested had to be addressed.
The outcome and its limits
A judgment was issued in the client's favour on appeal, and the outcome stood following consideration of the cassation challenge in the proceedings described here. This changed his legal position following the earlier judgments.
Recovery of the funds, however, remained dependent on enforcement proceedings. This distinction is central to the story: obtaining a favourable judgment does not establish that the money has returned to the client's account, nor does it mean that every related procedure has ended.
What you can learn from this story
A cheque signed in blank leaves considerable room for a dispute that may be difficult to contain later. If a judgment has already been issued, the review starts by identifying the available challenge procedure, its deadlines, the judgment's reasoning and the material documents, before assessing whether the outcome can be changed.

He paid for the car, then discovered a history he had not known when buying it
Companies and Contracts
Matter: a vehicle sale contract and prior insurance history
The client bought a car and paid the full price. Months later, he discovered information he had not had when entering into the contract: the vehicle had previously been classified by an insurer as a total loss following an accident.
According to the case summary, this information appeared neither in the sale contract nor in the material provided to the buyer. When he approached the seller, the seller denied responsibility. The buyer now had to explain why the car's history changed the assessment of the transaction he had agreed to.
The information that reopened the transaction
The dispute focused on the date of the insurance classification, the extent of disclosure, and its effect on the purchase decision and the vehicle's value. The claim required the information to be linked to the car's condition before the sale and its significance at the time of contracting to be demonstrated.
It was also necessary to assess the seller's defence about what he knew and whether the information had in fact been communicated to the buyer. The car's acceptable appearance at handover could not, on its own, answer those questions.
How the claim was built
The sale documents, insurance history, available technical evidence and correspondence were collected. These materials were then related to the time of contracting to show that the event predated the purchase and was material to the vehicle's valuation and the decision to enter into the transaction.
Organised in this way, the file explained what information the buyer had lacked and the basis of his request to rescind the sale. The review addressed the relevant documents and responses, rather than relying solely on describing the car as defective.
What the court ordered
The court ordered rescission of the sale contract and required the seller to take back the car and return the purchase price to the buyer. This is the judicial outcome described in this case study.
Enforcement of that obligation and proof that the buyer received the purchase price remain separate matters requiring verification. The judgment is therefore not presented here as evidence that the money was actually collected.
What you can learn from this story
Before buying a car, review the available technical and insurance history and related disclosures. If a material fact later comes to light, keep the evidence, record when it was discovered and retain the correspondence about it. Consider safety before repairing or otherwise dealing with the vehicle.

They came to collect payments, then found their names in a forgery case
Criminal Cases
Matter: vehicle transactions and allegations of forgery and misappropriation of funds
An employee at a car dealership asked people he knew to attend the company's premises and collect payments. According to the summary of facts, they complied out of personal trust, unaware that the transactions would later become the subject of allegations of forgery and misappropriation of funds.
After the events came to light, the person who had managed the transactions left the country, and several criminal complaints were filed. The clients' names appeared in the documents, the receipt of payments was an established fact, and some clients were in pretrial detention. The defence needed a precise explanation of those facts and each person's connection to them.
The names were there, but the roles were different
The transactions could easily appear to form a single chain involving everyone. Yet each client had a different relationship with the person who had asked them to attend, their own sequence of events, and limits to what they knew about the transaction.
An assertion of good faith alone was therefore insufficient. It was necessary to explain why each person attended and collected the money, and what the correspondence, documents and movement of funds established about the knowledge, agreement and participation attributed to them.
Distinguishing each person's role
The transactions, correspondence and movement of funds were compared, and each client's actions were examined individually. The chronology was reconstructed to establish who had requested the action, what the client had been told, and what they could have known at the time.
The legal work focused on providing an evidence-based explanation for the conduct reflected in the documents and examining the evidence relating to the elements of the allegations. These details allowed each person's role to be presented separately from the role attributed to anyone else.
The outcome in the matters described
Acquittals were issued in the matters covered by this case study. Each set of facts and stage of proceedings remains distinct: the outcome of one matter does not automatically extend to another complaint or a different incident involving the same person.
What you can learn from this story
A person's name appearing in a transaction, or their receipt of a payment, needs to be explained in the context of the evidence and circumstances. Keeping messages and setting out the chronology and actual role helps build that explanation. Relying only on the words “I acted in good faith” may leave essential questions unanswered.

He sold USDT, then faced an allegation linked to someone else's investment promise
Cryptocurrencies
Matter: a cryptocurrency transaction with civil and criminal proceedings
The client sold USDT through a trading platform, and the transaction records showed that the cryptocurrency reached its destination. He later faced an allegation and a financial claim connected with the buyer's dealings with another person.
That third party had promised the buyer trading activity and profits, and had asked him to buy the cryptocurrency and transfer it to them. When the buyer lost his money and filed a complaint, the seller's name appeared in the case. The client came to Summit after a criminal judgment had been issued against him, while also facing a civil compensation claim.
How two transactions became part of one dispute
The review needed to distinguish the client's sale from the investment promises attributed to the third party. Who sold the cryptocurrency? Who received it? Who requested the transfer? And who promised to manage funds or generate profits?
These questions mattered to the assessment of liability. Delivery of the cryptocurrency was one element of the file, but it could not, on its own, determine the parties' roles or the remaining elements of the allegation and claim.
The records that needed to be read together
Trade orders were linked to payment and transfer records. Platform data, transaction times, correspondence and the parties' identities were reviewed. The examination focused on the scope of the client's transaction and whether he had promised to trade or manage the buyer's funds.
Technical evidence helped explain the transaction trail and the limits of the seller's role more clearly. It was assessed alongside the other documents and facts, including the agreement, source of payment and relationship with the third party, as part of the work on both the civil and criminal proceedings.
Two outcomes that must be kept separate
The civil proceedings described here concluded in the client's favour. The technical records were also used in pursuing the options relating to the criminal judgment.
At the time the approved summary for this case study was prepared, review procedures were still under way in the criminal proceedings, with no final outcome announced. This story therefore makes no claim that the criminal judgment was overturned or that a final acquittal was obtained in those proceedings.
What you can learn from this story
In a cryptocurrency dispute, retain complete trade orders, payment and transfer records, and correspondence. Linking those records to identity, the agreement and each party's role is more useful than providing a single transfer screenshot, but does not by itself guarantee any particular outcome.

After six years of employment, the final settlement remained in dispute
Employment Law
Matter: review of a final settlement and employment termination entitlements
After six years in an administrative role, the client's employment was terminated, leaving a disagreement over the basis of his final settlement. He brought his employment contract, payslips, termination notice and assorted correspondence. The documents were available, but they did not provide a single clear answer as to the amount due or how it had been calculated.
Before deciding how to respond to the proposed settlement, the client needed to understand what belonged in the claim, what had actually been paid, and the basis for each deduction or difference in the calculation.
From a total figure to components that could be checked
The review covered the contract, basic salary, length of service, transfers and termination documents. The materials were arranged chronologically, separating the claim components from payments and deductions, and identifying where further documents or explanations were needed.
This separation made it possible to discuss each point on its own basis and identify the strengths and gaps in the file before negotiations or formal action.
What the client received
The engagement produced a statement of claims, the documents needed and options for handling the settlement. The result was a clearer understanding of the position and a plan for negotiation or a claim, rather than a court judgment or an announced recovery of funds.
What you can learn from this story
When reviewing an employment settlement, ask for each component and its calculation basis to be set out, and check any acknowledgement of receipt against what you have actually received. Combining all figures into a single total can conceal the point of disagreement and make it harder to discuss.

The goods were delivered; the promises to pay remained in the messages
Debts and Cheques
Matter: organising a commercial claim between two companies
A company supplied goods and services and sent its invoices, but repeated promises to pay went unfulfilled. It held messages, delivery evidence and documents supporting the dealings, yet each part was stored separately and no detailed contract covered the entire relationship.
The company needed to present a claim whose outstanding balance could be checked against what had actually been delivered and paid, while addressing possible objections concerning the invoices or acceptance of the supplies.
Reconstructing the sequence from scattered documents
Invoices were reconciled with delivery evidence, payments and correspondence. The outstanding balance, objections and gaps in the evidence were identified. The facts were then arranged to show how the debt arose, when it became due and what supported each part of it.
This organisation gave the company a clearer basis for discussing payment and assessing a formal claim. It also revealed what still needed to be completed, rather than leaving the other party with a collection of invoices that did not show the final balance.
The result described in this case study
The company received an organised claim file that could be used to assess negotiation or formal action. This case study does not describe a judgment in its favour or actual recovery of the debt: preparing a claim, obtaining a judgment and enforcing it are separate stages.
What you can learn from this story
Keep the records linking an invoice to delivery or acceptance of a service, and record payments, objections and written promises to pay. Organising these materials early helps define the claim and respond to disputes about it.

Questions about the published case studies
If a story resembles my situation, will the outcome be the same?
No. The type of dispute may be similar while the evidence, deadlines, obligations or procedural stage differ. A story helps explain how a matter is reviewed. Assessing your own situation requires its specific documents and facts.
Is every result shown here a court judgment?
Each story explains its outcome and its limits. Some examples describe a judgment; others concern advisory work or preparing a claim, and another set of proceedings may still be ongoing. An order to repay money also does not mean that it has actually been recovered.
Can a matter be reviewed after an earlier judgment?
The review begins with a copy of the judgment and its reasoning, the preceding procedures, service dates and the documents. What can be considered depends on the stage, deadlines and available procedures. A new document alone does not mean that every case can be reopened or a judgment changed.
Why are clients' names and images of their files not published?
To protect the confidentiality of their information and prevent identification through the details of the dispute. The website presents what is needed to understand the story, without publishing documents or identifying information merely to increase its marketing impact.
